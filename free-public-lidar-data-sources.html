Free Public LIDAR Data Sources in the US

Almost all US LIDAR coverage is public and free — the practical question is never "can I get this data," it's "how much manual GIS work do I want to do to use it."
In short US LIDAR elevation data is overwhelmingly public, funded through federal and state mapping programs, and free to download. Lidarman's search box fetches directly from the largest of these programs (USGS 3DEP) automatically, with no download or account required — this guide is for when you want the raw data yourself, or want to understand where Lidarman's numbers actually come from.

1. USGS 3DEP — the national baseline

The USGS 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) is the primary federal effort to acquire consistent, high-resolution LIDAR coverage of the United States, built up over years of coordinated federal, state, and local data acquisition. It's the data source Lidarman's search actually queries — type in a place name or coordinates and the pipeline geocodes it, pulls the relevant elevation data from 3DEP's web services, and processes it, with no manual download step or API key involved on your end.

Coverage and resolution vary by location — some areas have multiple LIDAR acquisitions spanning years, others have thinner coverage — but for most of the continental US, 3DEP is the right first stop, which is exactly why it's the default behind Lidarman's own search.

2. Getting raw data yourself

If you want the underlying files rather than a processed search result:

Lidarman's underlying pipeline (available as a scriptable CLI in addition to the web app) can ingest a downloaded LAS/LAZ point cloud or a DEM GeoTIFF directly, if you'd rather run your own processing on data you sourced yourself rather than a place-name search.

3. State and local portals

Many states run their own GIS data programs on top of the federal 3DEP baseline, and state-sourced acquisitions are sometimes newer or higher-resolution than what's reflected nationally — worth checking if 3DEP coverage in your specific area looks thin or outdated. Searching "[your state] LIDAR GIS data" or checking your state's official GIS clearinghouse is usually the fastest way to find it, since the specific program name and URL vary by state and change over time.

4. When raw data is actually worth it

For the common case — "is there anything interesting near this place" — going straight to raw data is usually more setup than the question deserves: a bounding-box download, a coordinate system to get right, and a GIS or scripting toolchain to turn raw points into hillshade/slope/relief imagery before you can even look at it. That whole path is what Lidarman's search collapses into typing a place name and waiting under two minutes.

Raw data earns its extra effort when you need something a per-search tool isn't built for: custom processing parameters, a very large area at once, non-US coverage (3DEP is US-only, so anything outside it requires a different source entirely), or feeding the data into your own separate analysis pipeline.

Skip the bounding-box download and coordinate-system wrangling for the common case.
